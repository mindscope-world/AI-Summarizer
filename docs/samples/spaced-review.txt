Spaced Review and Retrieval Practice

Students often prepare for examinations by reading their notes several times in the night before the paper. This feels productive, because familiar text is easy to read, but it is a weak way to remember. Two methods that work better are spaced review and retrieval practice.

Spaced review means returning to material at increasing intervals instead of all at once. A common plan is to review a topic one day, three days, seven days and thirty days after first studying it. Each return visit is short. Because some forgetting has happened by then, recalling the material takes effort, and that effort is what strengthens memory.

Retrieval practice means testing yourself instead of re-reading. After studying a section, close the notes and write down what you can remember, or answer questions about it. Then check what you missed. Studies of students in many countries find that this kind of self-testing produces better results on later tests than the same amount of time spent re-reading.

Used together, the two methods change how a study week looks. A student who studies a topic on Monday reviews it briefly on Tuesday, again on Thursday, and again the following Monday, each time starting with questions rather than with the notes. The total time may be similar to cramming, but it is spread out.

There are limits. Spacing takes planning, and the benefit is smaller for material that is only needed for a few days. Self-testing feels harder than re-reading, so students may wrongly conclude that it works less well. Teachers can help by providing questions and by scheduling reviews into the course rather than leaving them to individual discipline.

In summary, plan several short reviews of each topic over a month, and begin each review by trying to recall the material before looking at it.
